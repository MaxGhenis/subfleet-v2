# Winner

**Plan B, 84.0 to 74.0.** It wins because it is right about the thing the whole system is for: subfleet exists to spend several subscriptions, and it cannot see what a Claude subscription has left. B names the missing sensor at `bin/subfleet-claude:492`, proposes a specific mechanism to acquire it (lane homes with full logins under `CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR`), states honestly that the mechanism is unproven, designs the three-day experiment that settles it, and names the fallback if it fails. Around that it has the two assets that decide a one-maintainer rebuild: a 220-row invariant ledger that turns ten weeks of incidents into tests written before the code, and eight open decisions with recommendations. It also fits the operator's stack, protections, and CLI contract almost exactly, where A proposes Go, leaves desktop-login protection to a policy sentence, and never mentions the /tmp rule.

Plan A is the better *architect*. Its lifecycle section is the most rigorous prose in either document and it is the only plan that treats uncertainty as a state rather than an error. But it is a design for a system in general; B is a design for this system.

# Complementarity and A+B score

Adopting B as the plan of record, these are the specific things in A that would materially improve it, by A's heading:

**"Lifecycle, recovery, and cancellation"**
- The `indeterminate` attempt outcome, distinct from B's `lost`. B collapses "we know it died" and "we cannot establish whether it is still running" into one state; A's rule — "An indeterminate attempt cannot be silently treated as safely retryable" — is the difference between a safe retry and two writers.
- Quarantine with an explicit resolution path (inspect, reconnect, terminate-and-confirm, or operator override with actor and evidence recorded), plus "Elapsed time alone never releases an uncertain ownership claim."
- The cancellation/result-acceptance ordering rule in both directions: cancel-first keeps the later attempt's artifacts but cannot mark the job succeeded; accept-first makes cancel report already-finished. B's `kill` has no race rule at all.
- "Retain the account concurrency claim while that execution may still consume capacity" — directly relevant to B's lane-closure bookkeeping.

**"Workspaces, artifacts, and the limit of fencing"**
- Never reuse an uncertain attempt's write workspace; require confirmed termination before retrying in place. B's scheduler starts the next attempt immediately on a hard limit and re-adopts on daemon restart, with no such bar.
- The durable-publication sequence (temp file → fsync → atomic rename on the same filesystem → fsync directory → record hash), and `-o` as an export destination *reserved at admission* and published by rename. B's "written once by the daemon after the attempt ends" is the right policy without the mechanism.
- The paragraph beginning "**Fencing protects Subfleet's accepted state and publication path. It does not revoke a running CLI's filesystem or network powers**", including "must not advertise hostile-agent isolation" — B's never-rules guard and sandbox modes would be safer with this caveat written down.
- Result acceptance as a separate evaluation from process exit: "A nonempty answer and exit code zero establish less than 'the assignment is correct.'"

**"Routing and quota scheduling"**
- "Make the single-job rule for an unknown account an actual transactionally enforced reservation. An observed in-flight count is insufficient for two simultaneous callers." This is exactly the class of bug B observed at 06:31 and would reintroduce as `max_in_flight_per_lane` policy data.
- Fairness: an interactive allowance, per-parent concurrency bounds, and ageing so small urgent jobs cannot starve a large one. B has a global cap and nothing else.
- Concurrency reservations (enforceable) kept distinct from capacity reservations (estimates carrying uncertainty).

**"Approvals, external actions, and reset policy"**
- The typed external-action state machine `pending → executing → confirmed → failed → unknown`, with intent and operation key persisted *before* the call and "A timeout after submission means unknown until reconciled." B adopts Astra's persisted redemption id but has no general action model, and its gate section keeps semantics without adding reconciliation.
- "An expected-base preflight is not an atomic base lock" and "Status, explain, and dry-run operations must never redeem a reset, send a message, or launch an inference task."

**"Calling agents, notifications, and operator experience"**
- "'Sent to socket' is not 'read by the agent'" — separate `sent` and `acknowledged` states with event-ID deduplication, and a completion transaction that atomically records the accepted result and enqueues its notice. B has four delivery layers and `notice.ack`, but no rule preventing layers 1 and 2 from both firing, and no ordering guarantee between finalizing and enqueueing.
- "Delivery failures should not change a successful execution into a failed job."

**"Migration without losing the working fleet"**
- "The migration unit must be **a provider account and its execution handles**, not merely a named lane or model. Two apparently separate pools can still race over the same account's tokens and quotas." B's shadow week has no account-ownership rule; this single paragraph closes the largest hole in B's cutover.
- Step 6: import an active gate only if subject fingerprint, approvals, peer evidence and action state can all be verified; "Never rebuild approval from a summary."

**"Test strategy and release criteria"**
- The crash matrix — crash before and after each of admission, supervisor registration, provider launch, artifact rename, result commit, delivery send, and action submission, plus disk-full and spool exhaustion. B's crash coverage is one milestone-1 acceptance line.
- Numeric release gates (zero lost acknowledged jobs, zero stale-generation publications, reconcile within 30 s, a canary set plus a soak).

**"Delivery plan"** — the 8–12 engineer-week budget with per-milestone effort. B ships eight milestones with no time estimate at all; for a single maintainer this is the difference between a plan and a wish.

**"Configuration, distribution, and maintenance"** — reject new work before launching a provider when disk pressure makes durable acceptance impossible; reference-aware retention (never prune pending deliveries, unresolved actions, or gate evidence still in use), which B's "newest 500 jobs or 2 GiB, active jobs exempt" does not cover; and the warning that restoring an older database after a rollback can erase evidence of actions that already occurred.

**"Basis and limits of this assessment"** — the packaging finding. B freezes the public repo, which is defensible, but A's underlying rule ("Make the installed release the product tested in CI") applies to the private install too, where `~/bin/subfleet` today runs a checkout.

**A+B score: 35%.** A's contributions cluster precisely where B is weakest — dimension 2 at weight 20, where B scored 7, plus effort realism and crash-test rigour — and almost all of them are rules, states and test cases that graft onto B's daemon-and-SQLite design without contradicting a single one of its decisions, which is what makes the combination worth more than either alone. But they do not move B's load-bearing choices (lane homes, three-state honesty, the invariant ledger, policy-as-data, the open-decision list), and they leave untouched the three dimensions worth 35 points where B already dominates, so the marginal value is a substantial hardening rather than a second plan.
